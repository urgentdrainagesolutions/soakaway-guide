What Is a Soakaway? A Plain-English UK Guide

A soakaway quietly deals with the rainwater from your roof and driveway. When it fails, gardens flood and walls get damp. Here is how they work and how to tell when yours needs attention.

Quick answer: A soakaway is an underground pit or crate system that collects surface water (usually from roofs and driveways) and lets it soak slowly into the surrounding soil. It is used where there is no surface water sewer, and it must never take foul water from toilets, sinks or showers.

How a soakaway works

Rainwater runs from gutters and downpipes into an underground pipe, which feeds a chamber with plenty of empty space. Water is stored there during heavy rain and then drains away into the ground over the following hours. How well it works depends almost entirely on the soil: sandy and gravel soils drain well, while heavy clay, common across parts of the East Midlands, drains slowly.

UK rules for soakaways

  • Building Regulations Approved Document H says a soakaway should normally be at least 5 metres from a building
  • The design should be based on a percolation test, which measures how fast the ground drains
  • Soakaways are for surface water only; connecting foul drainage is not allowed
  • New soakaways on extensions or new builds usually need Building Control sign-off

Signs of a blocked or failing soakaway

  • Water pooling in the same part of the garden after rain
  • Gullies and downpipes backing up or overflowing
  • Damp patches on walls near downpipes
  • Water taking days, not hours, to disappear

Older rubble soakaways often fill with silt and stop working after 20–30 years. Our guide to a blocked soakaway explains the fixes.

What does a new soakaway cost?

A typical domestic crate soakaway, including excavation and connection, often costs between about £1,000 and £3,000, depending on size, access and how far the water needs to travel. A percolation test first avoids building one in ground that cannot drain.
